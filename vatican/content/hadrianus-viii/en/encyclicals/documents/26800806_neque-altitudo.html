ENCYCLICAL LETTER

NEQUE ALTITUDO

OF THE HOLY FATHER
HADRIAN VIII

TO BISHOPS, PRIESTS AND DEACONS, CONSECRATED MEN AND WOMEN, ALL THE LAY FAITHFUL AND ALL PERSONS OF GOOD WILL

ON THE LOSS OF SAINT BRIGID, ON THE EVENTS OF ANOMALOUS ANNIHILATION, AND ON THE HOPE AND RESPONSIBILITY OF CHRISTIANS BEFORE THE UNKNOWN IN CREATION

✠

INTRODUCTION

1. “Neither height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.”[1] When Saint Paul wrote these words, the Christians of the City of Rome were still a little flock, surrounded by a world they could neither understand nor resist. Today the Church is spread across hundreds of worlds, and the unknown that confronts us is far greater than anything Paul could have imagined. Yet not a single word of his sentence needs to be changed.

2. More than a year ago, the Archdiocese of Saint Brigid, together with all the inhabitants of that world, was lost to us. By the Apostolic Letter issued Motu Proprio “Lucerna ardens”, We have already provided for the juridical condition of those who are missing and of their families.[2] Yet law can answer only some of the questions. Many of the faithful, especially the sons and daughters of Saint Brigid who live far from home, are still asking other questions: What happened there? Where is God? What can we still do for them? How ought we ourselves to live? We write this Encyclical in order to answer these questions from the faith of the Church. We can answer little; but whatever We do not know, We shall honestly say that We do not know.

CHAPTER ONE · THAT DAY

3. Saint Brigid is a young world, and also an ancient one. Young, because its first settlers arrived there scarcely more than a century ago; ancient, because those settlers were for the most part descendants of the Irish, who carried a faith fifteen hundred years old out among the stars. From the rushes of that land they wove the crosses of Saint Brigid; on the first day of February they kept the feast of their patroness; over every door they hung a wooden tablet bearing the words “God and Mary bless this house”. The Archdiocese numbered forty-one parishes, two monasteries, one hospital and eleven schools.

4. At the moment when contact was broken there was no explosion and no alarm. The beacons of the spaceway ceased to answer; the last signal received by the relay stations was a routine weather report, and then there was nothing more. The observations of the Pontifical Academy and of the Federation have confirmed that this loss belongs to the same class as the anomalous annihilation events that occurred earlier at New Suva, at Shipyard 201 and at the Bond Ring. The last pastoral letter of the Archbishop of Saint Brigid reached us by mail ship three months after contact was lost. It spoke of the arrangements for Easter, and of the retirement of an elderly priest.

5. We know that, beyond Saint Brigid, there are many who wait each day for a letter that will not come. Some are students at Scorpius Centre who have become people who cannot go home. Some are sailors adrift upon the spaceways, whose wives and children were on that world. To you first of all We wish to say: the Church waits with you. Everything We shall say in this Letter is addressed, before all others, to you.

CHAPTER TWO · HEIGHT AND DEPTH

6. In the Letter to the Romans, Paul lists a series of things that might separate us from the love of God: death, life, angels, principalities, things present and things to come, powers, height and depth. Exegetes have long observed that, in the language of Paul's time, the words “height” and “depth” were often used of the rising and setting of the heavenly bodies, and of the powers that people believed held sway over the heavens.[3] Men then feared the stars, and thought that human destiny lay in the grip of invisible forces overhead. Paul answered them: whatever these forces may be, they are creatures; and the love of God is higher than they, and deeper too.

7. Today scientists call the cause of what happened at Saint Brigid an “anomalous annihilation entity”; the people have given it a name of their own, the “Lumberjack”. We know very little about it. Science tells us that it exists in dimensions we cannot directly observe; we can infer its passage only from the consequences it leaves behind.

8. What can the Church say about such a reality? The first thing the Church must say is one of the oldest phrases of the Creed: God is the “maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible”. The Apostle says: “For in him were all things created in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible … and he is before all, and by him all things consist.”[4] Whatever this entity may be, therefore, it does not lie outside the sovereignty of God. It is not a second god, nor another principle standing over against God as His equal. Many times in her history the Church has rejected such dualism, and she will not accept it today.

9. Nor should it simply be regarded as a demon. The Church believes that evil spirits truly exist, and that they make use of human fear; but the Church has never attributed to the devil everything that we do not understand and that does us harm. Earthquakes, plagues and the explosions of supernovae cause immense harm, yet they are not for that reason a willed evil. We know too little about the Lumberjack to say whether it has a will, still less that it bears malice. Until such things are known with certainty, the Church makes no such judgement; and We exhort priests and catechists likewise to refrain from making it.

10. Precisely because it is not a god, it may never be worshipped. We have learned that in certain places groups have already appeared which offer sacrifice to the Lumberjack and beg it to spare their worlds; and that there are those who claim to sense its coming and take money from the frightened. The teaching of the Church is clear: every attempt to master the future by means of occult powers is contrary to the worship owed to God.[5] Those who worship the Lumberjack set a creature in the place of the Creator. We ask the Bishops to lead back, with patience and firmness, those who have been led astray by such groups; for most of them are not wicked, only afraid.

11. Some preachers quote the words of John the Baptist, “the axe is laid to the root of the trees”,[6] and conclude from them that the worlds annihilated are the trees “that bring not forth good fruit”. This is a misuse of Scripture. The axe of which John speaks is the word of God calling men to repentance, not any created power. To say that the disappearance of a world was God cutting it down with His own hand is not faith but superstition.

CHAPTER THREE · NOT A PUNISHMENT

12. We must say it plainly: the loss of Saint Brigid is not a punishment from God upon those who lived there.

13. The disciples of the Lord Jesus asked the same question. Seeing a man blind from birth, they asked: “Rabbi, who hath sinned, this man, or his parents?” Jesus answered: “Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents.”[7] When He was told that the tower in Siloe had fallen and killed eighteen people, Jesus asked in return: “Think you that they also were debtors above all the men that dwelt in Jerusalem? No, I say to you.”[8] The Lord did not offer an explanation of the calamity; but He refused the explanation that treats the victims as sinners.

14. The people of Saint Brigid were no more sinful than the people of Scorpius Centre, of New Golgotha or of the Earth. Like us, they are sinners, and like us, they are redeemed. If anyone, because his own world still stands, imagines himself more pleasing to God than they, let him read the Book of Job again. Job's friends set out again and again to prove to him that his misfortune must have come from his sins; and in the end God said that those friends “have not spoken the thing that is right before me, as my servant Job hath”.[9]

15. Why, then, does God permit such things? To this question the Church has no answer that can take away every sorrow. The Catechism of the Catholic Church acknowledges that the existence of evil is a mystery to which no quick answer suffices.[10] What God did before Job was not to explain, but to lead him to look upon the vastness of a creation he had never seen: “Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?”[11] This was not mockery of Job but an invitation: creation is far greater than man imagines, and before it man must learn humility, and must learn trust as well.

16. The people named that entity the “Lumberjack”, and in that name there is fear, and also a little humility: before it, humanity is like the trees of a forest. Yet the Psalm tells us that God looks upon the just man as upon “a tree which is planted near the running waters”.[12] Trees can be cut down; but He who planted them remembers every tree.

CHAPTER FOUR · THE DUTY OF WATCHFULNESS

17. Faith does not bid us stand idly by. It is precisely because we believe that creation is the work of God that we believe it can be known; and precisely because we believe that man is the image of God that we have a duty to protect human life. Faith and reason are like two wings on which the human spirit rises to the contemplation of truth.[13] In the past the Church erred in her dealings with science, and she has acknowledged those errors.[14] Today she will not repeat them.

18. We therefore require the Pontifical Academy to take part, with the whole of its capacity, in the study of the anomalous annihilation entity, in collaboration with the scientific institutions of the Federation and with all researchers of good will. This participation of the Church is not meant to set up some explanation of her own alongside science, but to fulfil her duty of safeguarding creation. Our Predecessor Francis called the Earth “our common home”;[15] today that home has reached out among the stars.

19. Before Saint Brigid was lost, a scientist had foretold the danger. Several of her earlier forecasts had already come true, and people call her the “Prophet”. We wish to say two things. First, such prediction is science, not the charism of prophecy; the Church does not regard it as revelation, and she exhorts the faithful not to venerate the one who issues such warnings as a saint or a prophet. The Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith will publish a separate note on the distinction between scientific prediction and the prophetic charism. Second, although scientific prediction is not revelation, neither may it be disdained. To disregard a well-founded warning may itself be a grave fault. God has given man reason, and man has the duty to use it, above all when the lives of others depend upon it.

20. For this reason We have ordered the establishment of a commission, under Our direct authority, to examine whether, after the warning was given, the institutions of the Church at every level, including the Dicasteries of the Roman Curia and the local Church of Saint Brigid, fulfilled their responsibilities. Whatever fault belongs to the Church, the Church will acknowledge; whatever faithfulness her children showed in the hour of danger, the Church will also remember.

21. We wish also to address a few words to the Federation and to those who govern the several worlds. The danger of annihilation does not threaten one world alone, but all of us. In the arrangements for evacuation and relief, We ask you to remember three things:

  • No one may be placed in line according to wealth, nor according to the value of a world to the economy of the Federation. The poorest, the most remote, and those least able to save themselves must be cared for first.
  • Warnings must be given truthfully to the inhabitants who are threatened, and must not be withheld for fear of causing panic. People have a right to know what threatens their lives.
  • Do not abandon the frontier out of fear. The inhabitants of the periodic frontier have already given much for humanity's long journey; they must not be left behind in the face of a new danger.

22. We have learned that some, when the danger drew near, chose to remain where they were in order to care for those who could not leave: the sick, the elderly, those unwilling to forsake their homes. On such choices We pass no judgement. We remember only the words of the Lord: “Greater love than this no man hath, that a man lay down his life for his friends.”[16]

CHAPTER FIVE · HOPE FOR THOSE WHO ARE MISSING

23. Where, then, are the people of Saint Brigid? We do not know; nor does the Church know. On this point We will not say anything beyond what We know.

24. But the Church does know something else. In the Creed we profess that Christ “descended into hell”. On Holy Saturday Christ went to the farthest, deepest and darkest place that human beings can conceive. The Psalm says: “If I descend into hell, thou art present.”[17] The Apostle says that He who ascended above all the heavens is the same who first descended into the lower parts of the earth.[18] There is no abyss where Christ has not been, and no dimension beyond the reach of His love.

25. Therefore the Church prays for the people of Saint Brigid not as the dead but as the missing; not in despair, but in waiting. Our Predecessor John XXIV taught that a person may be struck from the civil register, but cannot be struck from the memory of God.[19] Today We add: with God, no name is ever lost.

26. In accordance with the provisions of “Lucerna ardens”, a lamp has been lit in the cathedrals of the window dioceses, to burn until the day when contact with Saint Brigid is restored. We ask the faithful everywhere, on the first day of February each year, to light a candle in their homes and to pray for the people of Saint Brigid and for all who are missing. We ask also the sons and daughters of Saint Brigid who live far from home: keep your crosses of rushes, keep your songs, keep the name of your homeland. The Church will keep them with you.

CONCLUSION

27. Today is the Feast of the Transfiguration of the Lord. The Lord “taketh unto him Peter and James, and John … and bringeth them up into a high mountain apart”,[20] and there the disciples beheld His glory. Afterwards they came down from the mountain and went towards Jerusalem, towards the Cross. So it is with the Church: on the heights she beholds glory, in the depths she passes through suffering. Whether in height or in depth, we never walk alone.

28. We entrust the people of Saint Brigid, and all who wait amid the unknown, to the Virgin Mary, Star of the Sea and Protectress of the Roman People, and to the intercession of Saint Brigid.

29. At the conclusion of this reflection, We wish to offer two prayers: one that all of us who believe in one Creator may say together; and one that Christians offer in the name of Christ.

A prayer for all who believe in God

Almighty God,
you created all things, visible and invisible;
the heights and the depths are in your hand.
Remember our brothers and sisters from whom we are cut off,
remember all who are waiting.
Drive out our fear,
that we may not worship what you have made,
nor judge others by their misfortune.
Grant wisdom to those who keep watch,
justice to those who govern,
and perseverance to those who wait.
Amen.

A Christian prayer

Lord Jesus Christ,
you descended into hell,
and there is no abyss where you have not been.
Look upon the people of Saint Brigid
and upon all whom we cannot find;
wherever they are, be there with them.
Keep your Church in faith amid fear,
in hope amid waiting,
in charity amid calamity,
until we are gathered together in your Kingdom.
Who live and reign with the Father and the Holy Spirit,
for ever and ever.
Amen.

Given at Golgotha, at the Apostolic Palace, on 6 August, the Feast of the Transfiguration of the Lord, in the year 2680, the fourth of Our Pontificate.
